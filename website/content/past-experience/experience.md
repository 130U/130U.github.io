##### Project 1: Scalable Expert Quality Control and Adaptive Review
*Co-developed methods for managing quality in large-scale annotation and expert production for a frontier foundation model company, covering more than 300 contributors across travel, food delivery, consumer goods, and e-commerce advertising. Defined operational rules for contributor reliability, acceptance, additional review, and dispute resolution, and used new adjudications and defect investigations to revise those rules. Audit records showed an improvement in the proportion of outputs that met all user requirements under the combined quality process. Work across projects also produced guidance for subsequent expert production programs.*
- **Turned contributor reliability estimates into an adaptive review policy.** Used Beta-Binomial posteriors, credible intervals, and verified sample counts to distinguish a short record of correct responses from sustained reliable performance. Combined these estimates with initial review status and risk requirements to determine additional checks. In one advertising workflow, flagged outputs received three additional reviewers, while outputs that passed initial review received one or two, with expert adjudication retained. New adjudications updated contributor records, feedback, and review intensity.
- **Examined how reviewer error patterns and task differences affect reliability estimates.** Compared confusion-matrix models, latent competence models, and dynamic Bayesian aggregation to assess whether overall accuracy represented performance across task types and production stages. Methods included [Dawid-Skene](https://doi.org/10.2307/2346806), [MACE](https://aclanthology.org/N13-1132/), and [dynamic classifier combination](https://www.robots.ox.ac.uk/~sjrob/Pubs/galaxyZooSN_simpson_etal.pdf). Operational judgments remained grounded in verified references, sample size, and task requirements, so disagreement alone was not treated as evidence of contributor error.
- **Established a reasoned basis for allocating additional review.** Compared Bayesian sequential decision methods, active relabeling, and cost-sensitive expert routing to determine which errors another judgment could resolve and what resources it would consume. Drawing on the distinct decision problems studied by [Chen, Lin, and Zhou (2015)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v16/chen15a.html), [ActiveLab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11856), and [DeCCaF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06906), translated these considerations into rules based on initial review status, contributor performance, risk requirements, and escalation to experts. Production teams could apply the rules and revise them as evidence accumulated.
- **Preserved independent random audits to detect problems outside the targeted review queue.** Fed audit findings, adjudications, and rework outcomes into contributor records and rule revisions. In the methodological analysis, distinguished active error detection from estimation of overall quality. [CROWDLAB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06812) addresses label quality, while [active statistical inference](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/zrnic24a.html) addresses estimation under selective verification; together, they clarify which questions different review records can answer.
- **Converted recurring disagreements into revisions of the standards.** Required reviewers to state their reasons, distinguished missing information from ambiguous criteria and defective submissions, and updated instructions, boundary cases, and expert calibration accordingly. Used criterion revision in [EvalGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272) and work on rating uncertainty by [Guerdan et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05965) as research comparisons when examining whether the rules supported consistent, defensible judgments.
- **Translated client-reported problems into defects that engineering teams could investigate.** Specified acceptance conditions for consistency across intent, location, dates, and landing pages. Connected each request to observed behavior, error categories, review reasons, and follow-up findings, and investigated geographic variation, session state, redirects, and page changes. The resulting records supported engineering diagnosis, revisions to review instructions, and subsequent checks.
- **Connected staffing and resource decisions to accepted output.** Used SQL and Python to join task, review, acceptance, time, and behavioral-event records, and reconciled platform activity with Hubstaff records. Analyzed accepted production and disputes against resource use to support contributor selection, workload checks, and targeted feedback. Distinguished outputs that met all user requirements from those that met only some, and assessed corrections through defect follow-up and random expert audits, preserving the measurement definition for the combined quality improvement.
- **Turned recommendation experiments into guidance for the conditions actually tested.** Distinguished exploratory browsing from immediate purchase, with separate definitions of dwell time, clicks, payments, and observation windows. Compared approximately 20 curated hotels with more than 100 eligible options, using logistic regression with decision-stage interactions, dwell-time transformations, and distributional analysis. The broader choice set increased browsing time more clearly than it increased clicks, informing guidance for the exploratory settings studied.

Specific review configurations and business experiments tested the conditions under which that process could transfer to other projects.

##### Project 2: Financial Task Design and Synthetic Data Engineering
*Led the design and production methodology for financial and business tasks at a frontier foundation model company, providing task specifications, reference cases, synthetic expansion strategies, and expert preference protocols for post-training and evaluation. Defined the task environment, or playground, and guided experts with doctoral-level subject expertise in constructing tasks. Used independent pilot production to determine whether the specifications were ready to scale. The company subsequently adopted Batch Zero for other new tasks, extending the method beyond the original project.*
- **Established a common basis for expert task design.** Defined target capabilities, available evidence, input structures, decisive variables, and acceptance conditions across finance, marketing, and related business decisions, and organized them in a scenario matrix. Experts used this specification to determine what a new task should test, where its difficulty should arise, and what evidence an answer needed to provide.
- **Led the development of reference cases, or golden cases, for both task construction and reviewer calibration.** Combined complete scenarios, input data, reference answers, reasoning, and scoring criteria. Guided experts in identifying which variables should change the conclusion and which errors should change the preference judgment. Required reference judgments to be traceable to evidence, calculations, and applicable rules; [FinQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.00122) and [FinRule-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11339) provide related research context.
- **Established rules for selecting synthetic data methods according to task structure.** Experts first specified which quantities could vary, which relationships had to remain intact, and which boundary conditions warranted testing. Generation methods were then chosen to expand a reference case across additional combinations of conditions while preserving a well-defined business question.
- **Preserved joint relationships among variables in static tabular tasks.** Used copulas and conditional sampling to generate related variables under specified scenarios, including company-level indicators. Used CTGAN, TabDDPM, and related generative models to expand records containing continuous values, categorical fields, and complex distributions. Method selection followed the data structure and dependencies the task required, with [SDV](https://docs.sdv.dev/sdv/sampling/conditional/single-table), [CTGAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00503), and [TabDDPM](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/kotelnikov23a.html) as methodological references.
- **Incorporated hard business constraints into input generation.** Used constrained sampling and variable transformations to enforce ranges, temporal ordering, and relationships among calculated indicators. Generated inputs were checked for both statistical plausibility and business validity, preventing individually plausible fields from forming an invalid case.
- **Developed path-generation methods for dynamic tasks such as price sequences.** Used stochastic differential equations and neural SDEs to represent processes evolving over time, with dependence and volatility specified according to the task. Neural parameterizations represented dynamics learned from data; related work includes [Kidger et al. (2021)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/kidger21b.html).
- **Generated conditional paths for tasks with known intermediate observations or endpoints.** Used Brownian bridges and stochastic interpolation to construct intermediate trajectories consistent with the information supplied in the problem. Distinguished conditional completion from forward path generation based on information available at the current time.
- **Required changes to inputs to trigger revalidation of reference judgments and preference data.** Checked business relationships and reference solutions after expansion, then generated model responses and commissioned fresh expert comparisons. Authors documented which conclusions remained valid and which changed. Managed inputs, reference solutions, and labels as dependent objects to prevent an old answer from being carried into production with new data.
- **Designed diagnostic cases to test shortcuts a model might use in judgment.** One ESG example contrasted a newer company with stronger substantive evidence against an older company with weaker evidence, testing whether the judgment followed operating and governance facts. The example illustrated a general requirement for task design: distinguish variables that determine the conclusion from surface attributes that can become misleading proxies.
- **Established a five-level pairwise preference protocol that captured substantive differences between responses.** Connected preference direction, strength, and reasons to evidentiary support, decisive variables, rule application, and consistency of conclusions. Used error types and boundary cases to calibrate experts and distinguish errors that changed the conclusion from weaknesses in presentation. [HelpSteer2-Preference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01257) and [Wu et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01693) provide research comparisons for preference strength and fine-grained feedback.
- **Created Batch Zero to test whether other experts could independently execute the full specification before production began.** Selected pilot contributors using their delivery records and asked them to complete task construction, inputs, reference solutions, and preference review. Used gaps in instructions, defective cases, and judgment disagreements to guide rework, expert instruction, and calibration. Expert pilot work and revision are also discussed in [MultiHiertt](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.454/).
- **Converted pilot findings into the conditions for Batch One and completed the operational handoff.** Delivered validated cases, expansion rules, review requirements, rework criteria, and escalation procedures, while retaining responsibility for task design and data strategy. Subsequent reuse of Batch Zero across tasks provided direct evidence that the method had value beyond a single delivery.

##### Project 3: Scientific Reasoning Evaluation and Task Design
*Developed a series of scientific reasoning tasks and acceptance methods for a frontier AI research laboratory, supporting model evaluation and post-training research. Translated knowledge from physics, mathematics, and risk engineering into testable capability requirements, producing original problems, complete solutions, and failure analyses. The work sought to reveal specific weaknesses in modeling, method selection, treatment of conditions, and argumentation, so that other experts could construct tasks, assess responses, and provide targeted feedback using a common methodology.*
- **Defined explicit capability specifications for the task series.** Set evaluation objectives around quantitative modeling, the combination of principles, compatibility of assumptions, and support for conclusions, then reflected them in problem conditions and reference derivations. Tied difficulty to identifiable reasoning demands, reducing reliance on surface complexity or model failure alone as measures of task value.
- **Made verification of the reference solution part of task acceptance.** Required solutions to explain modeling choices, decisive transformations, the domain of validity of each method, and the conditions supporting the conclusion. Revised problems when attempts to solve them exposed gaps. [Minerva](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14858) and [GPQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12022) provide context on quantitative reasoning evaluation and expert validation; verified solutions gave subsequent reviewers a common basis for judgment.
- **Established standards for arguments that admitted multiple valid solution methods.** Distinguished omitted conditions, incorrect models, formula misuse, calculation errors, and gaps in proof, and checked whether each decisive conclusion followed from its premises. Compared the applicability of symbolic computation, numerical verification, and formal proof to determine which claims computational checks could establish and which still required expert judgment.
- **Turned model failures into feedback tied to specific reasoning defects.** Identified where a derivation lost support, which conditions were missing, and what correction would make the argument valid, then used those findings in review and task revision. Kept final-answer correctness separate from the validity of the reasoning, drawing on process supervision in [Lightman et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050) and error identification in [ProcessBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06559).
- **Treated task difficulty and the ability to distinguish models as separate research questions.** Compared item response theory, multidimensional ability models, and representative item selection to examine whether different tasks revealed different model capabilities. [tinyBenchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14992) provides a reference for modeling ability and item parameters; the scientific validity of an original task remained grounded in its conditions, complete solution, and expert review.
- **Used invariants, boundary conditions, and changes in assumptions to guide diagnostic task design.** Compared controlled transformations, parameter variation, and distractor tests, distinguishing changes that should preserve a conclusion from those that required a new derivation. Related behavioral tests include [CheckList](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.442/) and [GSM-Symbolic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05229). Task construction focused on principles and relationships among variables, requiring the solver to establish a model before selecting formulas.
- **Introduced explicit checks on the conditions under which scientific methods apply.** For physics tasks, assessed modeling relationships, formula applicability, and computational correctness separately. For approximations and limits, checked that the assumptions supported the conclusion being drawn. [PhysReason](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12054) and [HARDMath](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09988) examine related difficulties; these checks subjected plausible-looking algebra to the scientific conditions it relied on.
- **Assessed feasibility, optimality, and attainment separately.** Required a candidate construction to satisfy the constraints, an independent argument to rule out better values, and a further determination of whether an admissible construction attained the bound. In one original kinematics task, used path invariance of vertical travel time to reduce the problem to horizontal scheduling. Proved that the minimum completion time was attained and the supremum was not, and derived harmonic-number asymptotics for staircase paths approaching the upper bound. Incorporated these results into the reference solution and acceptance conditions to check whether the problem demanded an unattainable outcome.
- **Delivered tasks, solutions, and error analyses that could support subsequent research.** Valid solutions made the proof requirements explicit; failure analyses identified what feedback needed to correct. Together, these outputs established a method spanning capability specification, task construction, solution verification, and review of model failures, providing a basis for further scientific reasoning evaluation and post-training research materials.
